### 3. Is this video AI-generated, manipulated, or a deepfake?
Use ALL available inputs — the attached video (including audio), images, model outputs, and eyeblink_data.csv. Synthesize visual findings with the numeric signals and frame-by-frame blink data below.

When weighing authenticity, apply these heuristics (none are definitive alone):
1. Eyewear and facial hair are often harder to fake convincingly — detailed, consistent glasses or facial hair can support authenticity, but their absence does not prove a fake.
2. Blink-cycle physiology (from eyeblink_data.csv): a normal human blink closes the eyelid for roughly 100–150ms, with a full open→closed→open cycle typically 300–400ms; blinks are sparse events (15–20/minute at rest), not a constant signal. At 15 fps (~66.7ms per frame), a single blink typically spans 2–6 consecutive "closed" frames bounded by "open" frames — that's the expected, benign pattern. Frames that alternate open/closed/open/closed continuously over many consecutive frames would require a blink cycle of ~66–130ms sustained repeatedly, which is not physiologically achievable even for fast voluntary blinks — treat sustained alternation as suspicious. That said, weigh isolated single-frame "closed" blips lightly: given the model's weak 56% closed-eye accuracy (see above), a scattered blip is more likely to be misclassification noise than a genuine anomaly — reserve suspicion for a longer, clearly-patterned oscillation run.
3. Missing/unknown frame patterns (from eyeblink_data.csv): these can mean the subject turned away OR that face detection struggled. Missing/unknown appearing in a group (e.g. during a head turn) is benign. Isolated single-frame instances of missing/unknown scattered between open/closed frames throughout the video are more suspicious and can indicate deepfake distortion — though genuine video edits/cuts can also produce this pattern, so weigh it alongside visual evidence.
4. Deepfakes are often easier when the subject stays still; natural head movement, expression changes, and interaction with the environment throughout the video can support authenticity.
5. Watch for unusual lighting, blur, warping, edge artifacts, teeth/eye irregularities, and temporal inconsistency across the video or within the blink sequence.
6. Speech/lip coherence (from the video's audio track vs. the subject's mouth): note whether the subject's lips are moving over the course of the video and whether coherent speech can be made out in the audio.
   - Lips moving + coherent speech: this is the expected baseline for a talking subject — genuine videos and well-made fakes both produce it (convincing forgeries are specifically designed to have synced, coherent speech), so treat this as neutral, not evidence of authenticity.
   - Muted or no audio throughout: also neutral — a common, benign editing choice (reposts, privacy, replaced background music), not suspicious on its own.
   - Lips clearly moving but no coherent speech can be made out: first rule out ambient noise, music, or speech in a language you don't understand but can still recognize as structured (coherent speech in any language counts, even if untranslatable) — if none of those explain it, treat this as a weak signal of a lip-sync mismatch consistent with manipulation.
   - Lips largely still while clear, coherent speech is present: can indicate a dubbed/voiceover mismatch — but weigh this carefully, since off-camera narration, reaction shots, or the subject simply listening rather than speaking are common innocent explanations.
